| | Traditional savings account | Axal |
|---|---|---|
| Typical rate | 0.5–5% APY, set by the bank | Variable, based on real-time lending demand — see [Where yield comes from](/docs/how-it-works/where-yield-comes-from) |
| Who holds your money | The bank | You — Axal is non-custodial, funds sit in your own wallet |
| FDIC/SIPC insured | Yes, up to applicable limits | No — Axal is not a bank; see [Risks](/docs/trust-safety/risks) |
| Withdrawal speed | Instant to a few business days | Crypto: minutes. Bank withdrawal: a few business days |
| What backs the yield | The bank's own lending and investing activity | Interest paid by borrowers on lending protocols Axal uses |
| Assets | USD only | USD, Bitcoin, and (coming soon) gold and tokenized stocks |

## The trade-off, honestly

A bank account is insured and boring by design — your balance doesn't move, and if the bank fails,
the government makes you whole up to the coverage limit. Axal is not insured the same way: your
yield depends on real lending markets, which carry smart-contract, market, and counterparty risk
that a savings account doesn't have. In exchange, you get a rate that reflects actual market
demand rather than a bank's set number, and you keep custody of your own funds the entire time.

Read [Risks](/docs/trust-safety/risks) before deciding how much to hold in Axal versus a bank.

## When a savings account might make more sense

If you need same-day, insured, zero-risk access to cash — for example, an emergency fund you can't
afford to see fluctuate at all — a traditional bank account is the more conservative choice. Axal
is built for balances you're comfortable earning a market-based yield on, understanding that yield
is not guaranteed and is not insured.
